\begin{abstract}
Residual-space diffusion models for single image super-resolution (SISR), such
as SRDiff and ResDiff, train a diffusion model to predict the residual between a
CNN's initial prediction and the ground truth. Because the residual is dominated
by high-frequency content, and because recent work has focused on strengthening
high-frequency guidance still further, the diffusion branch receives almost no
supervision on the \emph{global} low-frequency attributes of its output. ResDiff
explicitly identifies the consequence---a large color discrepancy when the model
is under-trained---and proposes ``utilizing a global color feature'' as future
work. We implement and study that proposal. We introduce a Global
Color-Consistency (GCC) loss: an auxiliary term that penalises the difference
between spatially pooled color statistics of the model's implied clean prediction
and those of the ground truth. The loss is training-time only, adds no
parameters, and leaves inference cost identical to the baseline. Across three
independent training runs we find the baseline's color error to be strongly
seed-dependent, ranging over $0.14$--$0.82$ under otherwise identical
configurations, whereas the GCC loss confines it to $0.15$--$0.26$ across five
paired runs spanning two datasets and an $8\times$ range of training-set size---a
standard deviation $6.5\times$ smaller. The loss is therefore better described as
\emph{bounding} color error than as reducing it: it recovers most of the gap on
runs that drift, and is approximately neutral on runs that do not. This single
behaviour also accounts for a DIV2K result that in isolation reads as a failure
to generalise, and survives an $8\times$ increase in training data with Stage 1
at full capacity, where absolute quality improves by $0.40$\,dB PSNR and $4.9$
FID. A gradient-level analysis shows the term's influence is not
uniform over the diffusion trajectory but concentrated at large timesteps by five
orders of magnitude; ablating that concentration removes most of the benefit,
identifying it as the mechanism rather than an artefact. We further show that no
admissible post-hoc color correction can match the learned solution, since such
corrections are bounded below by the accuracy of the only reference available at
inference. We report a consistent FID regression that we are unable to explain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Single image super-resolution is ill-posed: many high-resolution (HR) images are
consistent with a given low-resolution (LR) input. Adversarial
methods~\cite{wang2018esrgan} dominated early generative approaches but are prone
to unstable training and mode collapse. Denoising diffusion probabilistic
models~\cite{ho2020denoising} have since become the leading alternative, and
residual-space variants have proven particularly effective. SRDiff~\cite{li2022srdiff} and
ResDiff~\cite{shang2024resdiff} first use a CNN to recover the primary
low-frequency content, then train a diffusion model to generate only the
\emph{residual} between that prediction and the ground truth. Restricting the
diffusion model to the residual accelerates convergence and improves sample
quality relative to modelling the image directly.

This design has an under-examined consequence. The residual is, by construction,
mostly high-frequency; the standard $\epsilon$-prediction objective supervises
the noise added to it and therefore says little about the absolute color of the
reconstruction. ResDiff sharpens this further by adding frequency-domain losses
and high-frequency-guided cross-attention---improving detail while supplying no
additional color signal. The authors observe the resulting failure directly,
noting that ``the color will appear a large discrepancy when the model is
under-trained,'' attributing it to ``a lack of color features in the guided
high-frequency information,'' and proposing that ``utilizing a global color
feature may well address this issue in future work''~\cite{shang2024resdiff}.

We take up that proposal. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A Global Color-Consistency (GCC) loss} for residual-space diffusion
SISR: an auxiliary training-time term that supervises spatially pooled color
statistics of the model's implied clean prediction. It adds no parameters and
does not alter the sampling procedure, so inference cost is unchanged.

\item \textbf{A color-error metric} for SISR that measures precisely the
phenomenon the standard metric suite misses. We show empirically that PSNR, SSIM
and LPIPS can improve while color error worsens.

\item \textbf{A gradient-level analysis} showing that the auxiliary term's
influence is highly non-uniform across the diffusion trajectory---$0.002\%$ of
the primary gradient at $t{=}0$ versus $236\%$ at $t{=}T{-}1$. Loss magnitude is
a misleading proxy here, understating the term's true influence by roughly
$500\times$. Ablating this skew shows it to be the mechanism by which the loss
works, not an artefact: supervision of global color must act where global
structure is determined.

\item \textbf{The observation that baseline color drift is itself seed-dependent}
(spanning $0.14$--$0.82$ across identical configurations), which reframes the
contribution: the loss bounds a failure that may or may not occur on a given run,
rather than delivering a fixed improvement. This one behaviour accounts for our
CelebA and DIV2K results together, and implies that single-run comparisons of
color-consistency methods are unreliable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Generative super-resolution.}
Adversarial methods established generative SISR: SRGAN~\cite{ledig2017photo} and
ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} produce sharp textures but are unstable to train and
prone to mode collapse. Normalizing-flow approaches such as
SRFlow~\cite{lugmayr2020srflow} model the SR space explicitly at the cost of a
large model footprint.

\paragraph{Diffusion-based SISR.}
Denoising diffusion probabilistic models~\cite{ho2020denoising}, with the
sampling and schedule refinements of~\cite{song2021ddim,nichol2021improved},
avoid both failure modes. SR3~\cite{saharia2022image} adapts DDPM to SISR by
conditioning on a bicubically upsampled LR image.
SRDiff~\cite{li2022srdiff} introduces residual prediction, training the diffusion
model on the difference between the ground truth and an RRDB prediction rather
than on the image itself, which accelerates convergence.
ResDiff~\cite{shang2024resdiff} extends this with a frequency-domain information
splitter, high-frequency-guided cross-attention and FFT/DWT losses on the CNN.
The trajectory of this line of work is towards \emph{stronger high-frequency
guidance}; the low-frequency content of the output is left to the CNN and to the
residual formulation, and is never supervised directly. Our work addresses that
gap, which ResDiff itself identifies as open.

\paragraph{Color correction and color constancy.}
Correcting the color of an image is a long-standing problem, but the established
methods act either on the input or on the finished output. Reinhard et
al.~\cite{reinhard2001color} match per-channel means and standard deviations in a
decorrelated color space; Piti\'e et al.~\cite{pitie2005ndim} generalise this to
full $N$-dimensional distribution transfer; histogram matching is the classical
non-parametric equivalent. Computational color constancy instead estimates and
removes an illuminant~\cite{barron2015convolutional}, including for images that
were already incorrectly white-balanced~\cite{afifi2019color}. All of these are
corrective: they require a reference or an illuminant estimate at test time, and
cannot influence what the generative model learns. Section~\ref{sec:colortransfer}
shows this distinction is not academic---a post-hoc correction is bounded below by
the accuracy of the reference available at inference, and the learned loss
operates below that bound.

\paragraph{Loss weighting in diffusion models.}
How the diffusion objective is weighted across timesteps is known to matter.
Ho et al.~\cite{ho2020denoising} note that the simplified $\epsilon$-prediction
loss corresponds to a reweighted variational bound; Nichol and
Dhariwal~\cite{nichol2021improved} revisit the schedule and objective;
Choi et al.~\cite{choi2022perception} reweight to prioritise perceptually
significant noise levels; Salimans and Ho~\cite{salimans2022progressive}
introduce the $v$-parameterisation with its associated weighting; and Min-SNR
weighting~\cite{hang2023efficient} treats the problem as multi-task balancing
across timesteps. This literature concerns the \emph{primary} objective. We show
that an \emph{auxiliary} loss defined on the reconstructed $x_0$ inherits an
implicit and extreme timestep weighting from the $1/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ factor
in that reconstruction---spanning five orders of magnitude here---whether or not
its designer intends it, and that in our case this implicit weighting is
responsible for most of the term's effect.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Background}

Let $x_{LR}$ be the low-resolution input, $x_{HR}$ the ground truth, and
$x_{up}$ the bicubically upsampled LR image. A pre-trained CNN produces
$x_{cnn}$. Residual-space diffusion defines the target as
\begin{equation}
r = s\,(x_{HR} - x_{up}),
\end{equation}
with a rescaling constant $s$ chosen so $r$ occupies $[-1,1]$. The forward
process is standard:
\begin{equation}
r_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,r + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
\quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
\end{equation}
and the model $\epsilon_\theta(r_t, t, c)$ is trained with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}} = \mathbb{E}\big[\,\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(r_t,t,c)\|_1\,\big],
\label{eq:ddpm}
\end{equation}
where $c$ denotes CNN-derived conditioning features. Nothing in
Eq.~\ref{eq:ddpm} references the absolute color of the reconstruction.

\subsection{Global Color-Consistency Loss}

Figure~
Figure~\ref{fig:algo} states the complete modification. At each training step we
recover the model's implied clean residual using the standard closed form,
\begin{equation}
\hat r_0 = \frac{r_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\epsilon_\theta(r_t,t,c)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},
\label{eq:x0}
\end{equation}
clamp it to $[-1,1]$ (the range in which the target residual is constructed), and
map it back to image space, $\hat x_{HR} = \hat r_0 / s + x_{up}$. We then compare
spatially pooled color statistics:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GCC}} =
\big\| \, \mathcal{P}_k(\hat x_{HR}) - \mathcal{P}_k(x_{HR}) \, \big\|_2^2 ,
\label{eq:gcc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_k$ is adaptive average pooling to a $k \times k$ grid. Pooling
is what makes Eq.~\ref{eq:gcc} complementary rather than redundant: at $k=8$ it
discards texture and edges, retaining only the spatial color balance that
Eq.~\ref{eq:ddpm} leaves unconstrained. The total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{GCC}}.
\end{equation}

The clamp in Eq.~\ref{eq:x0} is essential, not cosmetic. Under a cosine schedule
$\bar\alpha_t$ reaches $2.4\times10^{-7}$ at $t = T-1$, so the reconstruction
amplifies by ${\sim}2000\times$; unclamped, we measured
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{GCC}} = 2.8\times10^{4}$ at $t=T-1$ against a DDPM loss of
${\sim}0.8$. Since $t$ is sampled uniformly, roughly one step in a hundred would
destabilise training.

\subsection{Timestep Weighting}
\label{sec:weighting}

The clamp bounds the loss value but not its gradient. Because
Eq.~\ref{eq:x0} carries the $1/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ factor, the term's effective
gradient scale at $t{=}T{-}1$ is ${\sim}2029\times$ that at $t{=}0$. We therefore
consider a reweighted variant
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GCC}}^{w} = \frac{\sum_i w_{t_i}\,\ell_i}{\sum_i w_{t_i}},
\qquad w_t = \bar\alpha_t ,
\end{equation}
which cancels the amplification and concentrates supervision at small $t$, where
the clean-image estimate is meaningful. We introduce this variant not as a
proposed improvement but as an ablation of the timestep skew itself;
Section~\ref{sec:snr} reports what removing it does.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate in a deliberately compute-constrained regime, matching the setting in
which ResDiff reports color discrepancy to be most visible. CelebA~\cite{liu2015faceattributes} is subsampled
to 3{,}000 training / 300 validation / 300 test images at $16\times16 \to
64\times64$ ($4\times$). Stage 1 is an RRDB with hidden size 16 and 4 blocks;
Stage 2 is a UNet with hidden size 32 and $2.28$M parameters, $T=100$ timesteps,
cosine schedule. Both stages train for up to 20{,}000 steps with early stopping
on PSNR. \textbf{Crucially, the baseline and proposed arms are identical in every
respect---data, schedule, seed, initialisation and Stage-1 checkpoint---except for
the presence of the GCC term.}

Early stopping monitors PSNR rather than color error: stopping on the metric the
method targets would bias model selection toward the proposed arm. Evaluation
loads the best checkpoint, not the final one.

\paragraph{Scope, and what these numbers may not be compared with.}
Our baseline is SRDiff. ResDiff motivates this work---it identifies the color
discrepancy and proposes a global color feature as future work---but we do not
reimplement it: the FD Info Splitter, the high-frequency-guided cross-attention
and the FFT/DWT losses on the CNN are absent here. Every comparison in this paper
is between SRDiff and SRDiff plus the GCC loss.

Consequently, none of our absolute values should be read against published
SRDiff, ResDiff or SR3 results. Those papers evaluate different tasks
($64\times64 \to 256\times256$ and $20\times20 \to 160\times160$ on CelebA,
$40\times40 \to 160\times160$ on DIV2K) with the full training sets and far
larger models; reconstructing $64\times64$ from $16\times16$ is not the same
problem, and our FID is computed over 300 images rather than a full test split,
which biases it upward. We make no state-of-the-art claim.

What the design does support is a controlled comparison. The two arms share
data, schedule, seed, initialisation and Stage-1 checkpoint, and differ only in
whether the GCC term is present, so any difference between them is attributable
to that term. The claims in this paper are of that form throughout.

\paragraph{Metrics.} PSNR, SSIM~\cite{wang2004image}, LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable} and
FID~\cite{heusel2017gans}, plus \textbf{color error}: the
mean absolute difference between per-channel RGB means of the generated and
ground-truth images, averaged over the test set. We also report LR-PSNR
(consistency between the downsampled output and the LR input), which measures
low-frequency fidelity and is therefore the quantity the GCC loss most directly
targets.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Evaluation is deterministic given a seed and both
arms are evaluated under identical seeds, so the comparison is paired on sampling
noise. We measured an evaluation noise floor by scoring a single fixed checkpoint
under four seeds (Table~\ref{tab:noise}).

The per-seed view (Table~\ref{tab:perseed}) shows something the means obscure.
The baseline's color error is \emph{highly seed-dependent}, ranging from $0.14$
to $0.82$ across otherwise identical configurations, and does not shrink
systematically with more data. CC-ResDiff's is not: it
falls between $0.15$ and $0.26$ in every run, with a standard deviation
$6.5\times$ smaller than the baseline's and a range $6.2\times$ narrower. The
apparent size of the improvement on any individual run is therefore governed
by how badly that run happened to drift.

We state the claim accordingly: \textbf{the GCC loss does not uniformly reduce
color error so much as bound it}. Where the baseline drifts badly the loss
recovers most of the gap; where the baseline is already accurate it neither helps
nor meaningfully hurts. The practical value is a guarantee rather than an average
improvement---the failure mode is prevented, not merely made rarer.

We deliberately avoid one tempting statistic. The correlation between a run's
baseline color error and the improvement CC-ResDiff delivers on it is $r=0.997$
over these four pairs, which looks compelling but is not evidence: improvement is
defined as $c_{\text{CC}} - c_{\text{base}}$, so $c_{\text{base}}$ appears on both
sides and the correlation is mechanically induced---it would be near unity even
if CC-ResDiff's output were pure noise. The variance comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:perseed} carries the same content without the coupling, and is
what we rely on.

On the remaining metrics we claim nothing. PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS differences
($-0.002$\,dB, $-0.0002$, $+0.0007$) are all smaller than their across-seed
standard deviations. In particular, the $-0.13$\,dB PSNR cost visible in our
first run does not survive replication; at $n=3$ the mean is $-0.002$\,dB. The
distortion cost we initially believed we had measured was seed noise.

FID is the exception: it is worse in $3/3$ seeds ($+3.90$, $+0.54$, $+3.65$).
That direction replicates even though its magnitude does not, and
Section~\ref{sec:snr} shows our explanation for it to be wrong.

\subsection{Scaling: More Data and a Stronger Stage 1}
\label{sec:scale}

The results above use 3{,}000 of CelebA's 162{,}770 training images and a
deliberately reduced Stage-1 network. To test whether the behaviour is an
artefact of that regime, we retrained at $8\times$ the data (25{,}000 images)
with Stage 1 restored to SRDiff's default capacity (32 features, 8 blocks;
$190$K $\rightarrow$ $1.48$M parameters). The test split is identical to the one
used above, so the two tiers are directly comparable rather than merely similar.

Absolute quality improves substantially (Table~\ref{tab:scale}). Stage 1 alone
gains $1.0$\,dB (PSNR $26.25 \rightarrow 27.25$), and the baseline diffusion
model gains $0.40$\,dB PSNR, $2.97$\,dB LR-PSNR and $4.9$ FID. The reduced-scale
setting was therefore genuinely limiting, and these are the numbers we would
recommend citing for the pipeline itself.

The GCC loss's \emph{relative} benefit shrinks accordingly, from $-55.9\%$ at 3k
to $-13.3\%$ at 25k. It is important not to read this as the method ceasing to
work at scale. CC-ResDiff's own color error is $0.2555$ at 25k, inside the
$0.15$--$0.26$ band it occupies at every other scale and on both datasets; what
changed is the baseline, which improved from $0.4494$ to $0.2948$ and therefore
left less to recover. The larger tier is a fifth confirmation of the bounding
behaviour, obtained under conditions chosen to break it.

Two caveats. The 25k tier is a single run per arm, so its individual deltas carry
the seed-noise caveat established in Section~\ref{sec:main}; given that baseline
drift varies sixfold by seed, one run cannot establish where within the band a
tier truly sits. And 25{,}000 images is still only $15\%$ of CelebA at
$64\times64$: we have shown the effect is not an artefact of the 3k regime, not
that it persists at the resolutions ResDiff reports.

The FID regression survives scaling ($+0.57$ at 25k), in the same direction as
all three 3k seeds and now $4/4$ across every CelebA configuration tested.

\subsection{Per-Image Consistency and Effect Size}
\label{sec:persample}

The aggregate improvement could in principle be produced by a handful of
outliers. It is not: CC-ResDiff has lower color error than the baseline on
$298$ of $300$ test images ($99.3\%$), with a mean per-image reduction of
$0.578$. The effect is systematic rather than distributional.

We are explicit about its magnitude. Color error is expressed in $0$--$255$
intensity units, so the reduction from $0.82$ to $0.24$ is a sub-one-level shift
in mean channel intensity. It is far above measurement noise (Table~\ref{tab:noise}
gives $\sigma = 0.0022$) and consistent across essentially every test image, but
it is close to the threshold of visual perceptibility. Figure~\ref{fig:qual}
therefore shows a measurable rather than a dramatic difference, and we do not
claim otherwise.

This is a consequence of the operating point, not of the method. ResDiff reports
color discrepancy as ``large'' when a model is under-trained; our baseline, though
compute-constrained, is trained to convergence with early stopping and exhibits
correspondingly mild drift. The regime in which drift is visually objectionable is
precisely the regime in which we expect the loss to matter more, and quantifying
that relationship is left to future work.

\subsection{Comparison with Color Transfer}
\label{sec:colortransfer}

A natural objection is that post-hoc color correction might achieve the same
effect for free. We apply per-channel mean and mean/standard-deviation
(Reinhard-style) matching to the baseline's outputs, using the bicubically
upsampled LR image as reference---the only color reference available at inference
time. Matching to the ground truth would trivially minimise our metric while
being unusable in practice, and is therefore inadmissible.

Neither variant helps (Table~\ref{tab:ct}). Both make color error \emph{worse},
and matching the standard deviation additionally destroys LR-consistency,
costing $7.4$\,dB of LR-PSNR: rescaling per-channel variance alters the image's
low-frequency content, which is exactly what LR-PSNR measures.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Method & ColorErr $\downarrow$ & LR-PSNR $\uparrow$ & PSNR $\uparrow$ \\
\midrule
Baseline                & 0.8234 & 43.690 & 25.095 \\
\; + mean transfer      & 0.8746 & 43.558 & 25.064 \\
\; + mean/std transfer  & 0.9333 & 36.243 & 24.915 \\
\midrule
\emph{reference floor} ($x_{up}$ vs $x_{HR}$) & \emph{0.4358} & -- & -- \\
\midrule
CC-ResDiff (ours)       & \best{0.2423} & \best{44.509} & 24.967 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Post-hoc color transfer against learned supervision, CelebA, 300 test
images. Post-hoc correction is bounded below by the color error of the reference
it matches to; CC-ResDiff operates $1.8\times$ below that bound.}
\label{tab:ct}
\end{table}

The reason is structural rather than incidental. Any post-hoc correction can be
no more accurate than the reference it matches to, and the only reference
available at inference---the upsampled LR image---itself differs from the ground
truth by a color error of $0.4358$, measured over the same test set. That value
is a hard floor on what post-processing can achieve. CC-ResDiff reaches $0.2423$,
$1.8\times$ below the floor, because supervision is applied during training
against the true HR image; the learned model does not need a test-time reference
at all. This is the central argument for a learned solution over a corrective
one.

\subsection{Timestep-Reweighted Variant: A Refuted Hypothesis}
\label{sec:snr}

The gradient analysis suggested a hypothesis: if the GCC term acts predominantly
at large $t$, and if that is what pulls global tone toward the dataset mean, then
cancelling the skew with the weighting of Section~\ref{sec:weighting} should
remove the FID regression while preserving the color benefit. The reweighting is
effective as an intervention---it takes the ratio of the effective gradient scale
at $t{=}T{-}1$ versus $t{=}0$ from $2029\times$ down to $0.0005\times$.

The hypothesis is wrong, in an informative way (Table~\ref{tab:snr}). Cancelling
the skew does not fix FID; it makes FID \emph{worse} ($77.81$ versus $73.23$),
while simultaneously destroying most of the color benefit ($0.673$ versus
$0.242$) and eliminating the LR-PSNR gain entirely. The reweighted variant is
inferior to the unweighted one on every metric we measure.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Variant & ColorErr $\downarrow$ & LR-PSNR $\uparrow$ & PSNR $\uparrow$ & FID $\downarrow$ \\
\midrule
Baseline                       & 0.8234 & 43.690 & \best{25.095} & \best{69.32} \\
CC-ResDiff ($w_t{=}1$)         & \best{0.2423} & \best{44.509} & 24.967 & 73.23 \\
CC-ResDiff ($w_t{=}\bar\alpha_t$) & 0.6728 & 43.366 & 24.963 & 77.81 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Ablating the timestep skew. Removing the concentration at large $t$
removes most of the benefit, showing that the skew is the mechanism of action
rather than an artefact. It also fails to fix FID, refuting our hypothesis about
the regression's cause.}
\label{tab:snr}
\end{table}

Two conclusions follow, and we consider the second more valuable than a
confirmation would have been.

First, \textbf{the concentration at large $t$ is the mechanism, not a defect}.
This is coherent in hindsight: in a diffusion model the large-$t$ steps determine
global, low-frequency structure while small-$t$ steps refine local detail. A loss
on \emph{global color} should therefore act at large $t$, and an implementation
that spreads it uniformly over the trajectory supervises color where color is no
longer being decided. Practitioners adding auxiliary losses on the reconstructed
$x_0$ should treat the induced timestep weighting as a design parameter to be
measured and reasoned about, not as an accident to be normalised away.

Second, \textbf{the cause of the FID regression remains unexplained}. Our
mechanism-based account is falsified by this experiment, and we do not have a
replacement. Reporting a plausible story we had not tested would have been easy;
testing it cost one training run and showed it to be wrong. We flag the open
question rather than leaving an untested explanation in the literature.

\subsection{Gradient Analysis}

Loss values suggest the GCC term is negligible---$0.06\%$ of the DDPM loss at
$\lambda=0.1$. Measuring parameter gradients on a trained checkpoint tells a
different story (Table~\ref{tab:grad}): the ratio is $33.9\%$ averaged over
uniformly sampled $t$, roughly $500\times$ larger than the loss ratio implies.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
$t$ & $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GCC}}$ & clamped & $\lambda\|\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{GCC}}\| / \|\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}}\|$ \\
\midrule
0        & $1.2\times10^{-5}$ & 0.4\%  & $0.002\%$ \\
25       & $2.8\times10^{-5}$ & 0.2\%  & $0.080\%$ \\
50       & $5.4\times10^{-5}$ & 0.1\%  & $0.398\%$ \\
75       & $8.4\times10^{-5}$ & 0.1\%  & $1.276\%$ \\
99       & $3.4\times10^{-2}$ & 97.4\% & $\mathbf{235.7\%}$ \\
uniform  & $9.8\times10^{-4}$ & 1.5\%  & $\mathbf{33.9\%}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The GCC term's gradient contribution spans five orders of magnitude
across the trajectory. At $t{=}T{-}1$ nearly all elements saturate the clamp, yet
the remainder still carry the ${\sim}2000\times$ amplification.}
\label{tab:grad}
\end{table}

This explains the ablation's cost structure: at $\lambda=0.5$ the auxiliary
gradient would reach ${\sim}170\%$ of the primary one, so the denoising objective
is crowded out and all metrics degrade.

It also suggested an explanation for the FID regression---a term acting
predominantly at large $t$ pulling global tone toward the dataset
mean---which we tested directly by cancelling the skew. That explanation turns
out to be wrong, and the experiment instead shows the skew to be the source of
the method's benefit; we present it in Section~\ref{sec:snr} rather than here,
since the result reverses the reading one would naturally take from
Table~\ref{tab:grad}.

More generally, the phenomenon is a property of \emph{any} auxiliary loss defined
on the reconstructed $x_0$, not of ours specifically. Such a term inherits the
$1/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ factor and is therefore implicitly weighted towards large
$t$ by orders of magnitude, whether or not its designer intended it. Since
Section~\ref{sec:snr} shows this weighting can be responsible for most of a
term's effect, we recommend measuring and reporting the gradient ratio---not the
loss ratio, which understates it here by ${\sim}500\times$---whenever such a term
is introduced.

\subsection{DIV2K: The Same Behaviour, Not a Failure to Generalise}

We repeated the experiment on DIV2K~\cite{agustsson2017ntire} at matched scale (Table~\ref{tab:div2k}).
Taken alone, the result reads as a failure to generalise: every sign flips
relative to the CelebA means.

\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & Baseline & CC-ResDiff & $\Delta$ \\

Read against Table~\ref{tab:perseed}, however, it is not a separate phenomenon.
The DIV2K baseline's color error is $0.165$---squarely in the low-drift regime,
essentially identical to the CelebA seed ($0.140$) on which CC-ResDiff also
provided no benefit; Figure~\ref{fig:qual_div2k} shows the two arms are
visually indistinguishable here. DIV2K falls exactly where the CelebA seeds predict: with no
drift to correct, the loss neither helps nor meaningfully hurts, and CC-ResDiff's
own color error ($0.204$) sits in the same narrow band it occupies everywhere
else ($0.15$--$0.26$).

The DIV2K result is therefore better understood as one of five data points
consistent with the bounding behaviour of Section~\ref{sec:main} -- alongside
the scaling tier of Section~\ref{sec:scale} -- rather than as a failure to
transfer across datasets. It is also consistent with ResDiff's observation that
the discrepancy appears when a model is under-trained: the DIV2K Stage-1 network
is comparatively strong here (LR-PSNR $47.2$ versus $44.7$), leaving less for the
diffusion branch to get wrong.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{qualitative_div2k.png}

We nevertheless report it as a limiting case, because it defines the method's
scope operationally. A practitioner should expect GCC supervision to pay for
itself only where the uncorrected model actually drifts---and, given that whether
it drifts varies by seed, the argument for applying it is insurance against a
failure that may or may not occur in any particular run, not a guaranteed gain.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scale.} Our main results use 3{,}000 training images at
$64\times64$; Section~\ref{sec:scale} repeats the experiment at 25{,}000 images
with Stage 1 at full capacity and finds the bounding behaviour intact. Both
remain far below the resolutions and model sizes used by SRDiff and ResDiff, so
whether the effect persists there is untested.
\item \textbf{Sample size.} Three training runs per arm on CelebA, one on DIV2K.
Three seeds are enough to establish that the baseline's drift varies widely and
that CC-ResDiff's does not, but not to characterise the distribution of either.
The FID regression replicates in direction across all three seeds; its magnitude
does not.
\item \textbf{No DIV2K noise floor}, so the $+23.4\%$ regression there is not
established as statistically real.
\item \textbf{FID sample counts are small} (300 CelebA, 512 DIV2K), biasing FID
upward in absolute terms; only the relative comparison is meaningful.
\item \textbf{Drift is not predicted in advance.} We show the method helps where
color error is high, but offer no way to determine beforehand whether a given
training configuration will drift.
\item \textbf{Effect size at this operating point is small in absolute terms.}
The color-error reduction is a sub-one-level shift in mean channel intensity:
systematic ($298/300$ images) and far above measurement noise, but near the limit
of visual perceptibility. Whether the benefit grows in the more severely
under-trained regime ResDiff describes is untested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We implemented the global color feature that ResDiff identified as future work,
and in characterising it arrived at a different claim than we set out to make.
The GCC loss is simple and parameter-free at inference, and it does reduce color
drift substantially---but its reliable effect is to \emph{bound} color error
rather than to lower it by some fixed amount. The baseline's drift varies by a
factor of six across random seeds; the loss confines it to a narrow band in every
run we performed, on both datasets. What it buys is predictability.

Three results were not what we expected. The apparent distortion cost we measured
at $n=1$ did not survive replication and was seed noise. The extreme implicit
timestep weighting that an auxiliary loss on the reconstructed $x_0$ inherits
turned out, when ablated, to be the source of the benefit rather than a flaw:
supervision of global color must act at large $t$, where global structure is
decided. And our mechanism-based explanation of the accompanying FID regression
was falsified by that same ablation, leaving the regression unexplained.

The methodological implication is broader than our loss. Because baseline color
drift is seed-dependent by a factor of six, any single-run comparison of
color-consistency methods---including the one we ourselves first
reported---can overstate or understate an effect several-fold. We would encourage
reporting per-seed color statistics rather than aggregate improvements.
Predicting in advance which configurations drift, and testing whether the benefit
grows in the more severely under-trained regime ResDiff describes, are the
natural next steps.
